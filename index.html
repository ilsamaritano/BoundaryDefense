One cheap drone can shut an airport.

Gatwick, December 2018: roughly 1,000 flights grounded over 33 hours. Abqaiq–Khurais, 2019: Saudi crude output temporarily halved. Consumer multirotors cost a few hundred dollars and slip under low-altitude radar.

Radar loses small targets near the ground, RF analysis is blind to autonomous drones, and cameras struggle at night and in fog. Passive acoustic sensing fills the gap: every multirotor makes noise, and listening needs no emitted signal and is largely unaffected by weather. But ground-mounted arrays are fixed and reach only 300–500 m at best.

This paper asks a different question: what if the microphones fly?

Fly faster, hear less.

A patrolling drone covers more of the boundary, but its own rotors get louder as it speeds up. Since fleet size scales with the inverse square of detection range, lost range is costly. The authors call this the Defender's Dilemma.

The defender's dilemma

Sensor gets noisier as it moves

Forward flight raises ego-noise. Spectral subtraction against a fingerprint of the drone's own noise cuts the floor by 13.0 dB, stretching the range from about 2 m to 9.1 m for a Mavic Air 2 at 28.8 km/h.

Halve the detection range and you need four times the drones to tile the wall.

The attacker's dilemma

Fast is loud, slow is exposed

Every intruder archetype has one speed that minimizes its chance of being heard. Go slower and dwell time inside range goes up. Go faster and the source level grows faster than exposure shrinks.

A fixed-wing VTOL keeps detection below 40% at its best speed. A racing FPV drone is heard over 90% of the time at any speed.

Size a static grid in seconds.

The paper tiles the wall with spacing D = 1.6 × Rdet (20% footprint overlap) and counts N = ⌈Wy/D⌉ × ⌈(Wz−zmin)/D⌉. Change the detection range or wall and watch the quadratic law bite.

The fastest patrol is not the cheapest.

Measured on a Mini 3 Pro, power rises from about 25 W at hover to 45 W at 35 km/h, and endurance falls from 42.6 to roughly 23 minutes. Add battery rotation for 24-hour coverage and the fleet-minimizing speed lands far below the detection optimum.

Cost, honestly

At the energy-optimal speed the airframe bill for a 500 × 200 m boundary is under $12,000 (about 30 Mini 3 Pro units at roughly $400). That is hardware only. It excludes deployment, operations, charging infrastructure, and the response system.

Rule of thumb from the paper: short high-alert missions patrol near 30–35 km/h; sustained perimeter duty patrols near 9.2 km/h.

How the model was built.

  1. Fly real drones

    A Mavic Air 2 intruder flew straight passes at 0–47.2 km/h while two Mini 3 Pro defenders hovered at 5.4–5.9 m with onboard microphones. 390 s of intruder flight produced 3,904 synchronized telemetry frames.

  2. Cancel the drone's own noise

    Build a spectral fingerprint of the defender's ego-noise from clean segments, then subtract it (Ŝint = max(Srx − βŜego, δŜego)). Net reduction: 13.0 dB. Detection range grows about 4.5×.

  3. Fit an acoustic model

    Source level grows with speed up to a plateau; sound spreads with distance and atmospheric absorption. A drone detects when received level minus its ego-noise floor exceeds an SNR threshold of 3 dB. Validation: ROC AUC 0.91, mean range error 1.14 m.

  4. Simulate the wall

    Defenders sit on a gap-free grid with spring-tethered Brownian position noise (σ = 0.3 m). A vectorized NumPy engine runs about 19,000 replications per second per core across straight, zigzag, evasive, and altitude-change intruder trajectories.

What this does not claim.

Results come from a calibrated simulation of a specific hardware pairing. Read them as sizing guidance, not a field-proven system.

  • Calm, open-air conditions. Wind, rain, humidity, and urban noise (55–75 dB(A)) would shrink detection range and raise fleet needs.
  • Detection only. No jamming, capture, or other response is modeled.
  • Zero-knowledge attacker. An adversary who times a crossing at a coverage gap, or floods the area with decoy emitters, needs a game-theoretic treatment.
  • Crossing ambiguity. A single-altitude wall cannot tell an intruder that crossed from one that approached and turned back.
  • Multi-defender interference. Friendly-drone noise in close formation is handled with fingerprint whitelisting, but its effect on range is future work.
  • Single-platform calibration. One intruder and one defender model were measured; the other archetypes use parametric extrapolation.

Questions people ask.

Can drones detect other drones by sound?

Yes. Every multirotor radiates broadband propeller noise, and it is detectable passively. The difficulty on a flying platform is ego-noise from its own rotors. Spectral subtraction against a calibrated fingerprint cut the noise floor by 13 dB in these experiments.

How many drones does it take to protect a 500 × 200 m perimeter?

About 403 hovering drones seal the wall with no gaps. Patrolling at 35.4 km/h, a fleet roughly ten times smaller reaches 99% detection. For continuous 24-hour operation the paper's energy-optimal setup uses about 20 airborne drones and about 30 in total.

What is the best patrol speed?

It depends on mission length. Detection crosses 95% at 29.4 km/h and 99% at 35.4 km/h, so short high-alert missions favor 30–35 km/h. Sustained missions favor 9.2 km/h to keep battery swaps, and therefore total fleet, low.

Why does a 6 dB quieter intruder double the fleet?

A quieter source is audible from less far away, so each defender's footprint shrinks and more of them are needed. In the simulations the 99% fleet rose from 53 to 110 at an intruder speed of 25 km/h.

Does it work in wind or in a city?

Not established. All experiments were done in calm, clear, open-air conditions. Ambient urban noise and wind would reduce detection range, and the authors list weather-dependent bounds as future work.

Where are the code and data?

At github.com/ilsamaritano/BoundaryDefense. The paper notes that access is for reviewers for now, with a Zenodo release planned upon acceptance.
